Pond day: will the ice hold by Saturday?

    Built for a laptop or a projector. On a phone, turn it sideways for a bigger picture.

    For teachers: what this page is

    One idea, shown

    This is a prototype of a different way to build a MathLab tool. It covers one skill: writing a transformed parent function, y = a·√(x − h), from data and using it to predict. It is meant to sit beside your lesson, before or after it. It does not replace the lesson.

    What students do

    • See it their own way first. They draw a prediction before any math, then look at three other ways of seeing the same four dots.
    • Cause the change. They drag the curve to slide it and stretch it. The equation rewrites itself while they drag, in matching colors.
    • Watch the formula make the picture. Evaluating the equation is three visible moves: slide, root, stretch. Solving it backward undoes the same three moves in reverse order.
    • Tell the story. They finish by writing the story of the math and can copy it into Canvas.

    Ways to use it

    • Projected: use the chapter buttons to jump. The "In class" lines are short prompts for the room.
    • On laptops: about 10 minutes, alone or in pairs.
    • Homework: students paste their story into a Canvas text entry.

    The model, and its limits

    New ice forms on the underside of the sheet, and heat has to leave through the ice already there. Under steady cold with no snow cover, thickness grows in proportion to the square root of time (Stefan's law of ice growth, 1891). The measurements here are made up to follow that law exactly, with a factor of 1.3 inches per square-root day. Real ponds are messier: warm spells, snow, currents and springs all change it.

    Four inches of new, clear ice is the usual minimum for walking that state natural-resource agencies publish. It is a guideline for a math problem, not safety advice. Real ice is never guaranteed safe.
